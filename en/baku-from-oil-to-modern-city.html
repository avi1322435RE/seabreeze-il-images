Baku: From Oil Town to Modern Metropolis

History, infrastructure, urban renewal and real estate

OHAYON PROPERTIES | BAKU · Updated November 2026

Introduction: the story behind the skyline

Anyone visiting Baku today sees a city with two faces. In the historic heart are stone walls, alleys and buildings recalling centuries of trade between the Caucasus, Persia and the Caspian. Not far away stand glass towers, cultural centres and hotels. This look is not the result of overnight change: it reflects layers of economic, political and urban development, from the oil boom of the nineteenth century, through the Soviet era, to today's independent state. Understanding this process matters both to travellers and to anyone examining the local property market. A city that looks impressive in a photo can contain large gaps between neighbourhoods, infrastructure and types of housing demand.

Oil: the first engine of growth

The Baku area has been known for centuries for its oil and natural gas. In the nineteenth century, as drilling and industrial technologies developed, oil production expanded and the city attracted workers, engineers, traders and investors. New wealth changed the built environment: large residences, public institutions and commercial buildings rose alongside industrial facilities. European architectural influences from this boom period can still be seen in the city centre. But there is another side to the story: an economy based on a natural resource is exposed to cycles. When energy prices change, the effect can reach state revenues, infrastructure budgets and investment decisions. So the history of oil explains not only Baku's wealth but also why economic diversification is seen as a key goal in the country.

Baku in the Soviet period and after independence

During the twentieth century Azerbaijan was part of the Soviet Union. Baku developed as an industrial and administrative centre, and residential neighbourhoods and infrastructure suited to the needs of the time were built. Much of today's urban fabric reflects those decades. With independence in 1991 the country had to reshape its economic institutions, trade ties and sources of finance. The years after independence were not uniform economically or socially, so it is wrong to describe the whole city as one block. Older districts, business quarters, new neighbourhoods and coastal developments operate in different local markets. For an investor in a specific property, it is important to know the immediate surroundings rather than rely on macro data for the whole country.

The Flame Towers and the symbols of the new Baku

The Flame Towers are among the best-known symbols of the modern city. Their shape recalls flames, a motif with a place in local culture, and their illuminated facades have become part of Baku's night-time look. The buildings serve various purposes, including residences, a hotel and offices. Another architectural symbol is the Heydar Aliyev Center, designed by Zaha Hadid. Its flowing lines differ from the traditional geometry of many buildings in the city. These buildings show that Azerbaijan sought to present an international, contemporary urban identity. At the same time, an iconic building is not the only measure of urban success. Quality of life also depends on transport, maintenance, education, public spaces and access to everyday services.

The Old City: heritage alongside renewal

Baku's Old City, Icherisheher, shows the deepest layer of history a visitor can experience in the centre. The area, which includes the Maiden Tower and the Palace of the Shirvanshahs, was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000. Such status gives weight to conservation, but it also highlights the tension between urban development and protecting cultural assets. In growing cities there is sometimes pressure to add floor space, expand infrastructure and attract tourists. Good planning must also take heritage and public space into account. For visitors, the historic area is a reminder that Baku is not only a destination of towers and malls; it has a long urban tradition on which its modern identity was built.

The boulevard and the Caspian: a new relationship with the coast

Baku's coastline plays a central role in the city's identity. The promenade, known as Baku Boulevard, is used for meeting, walking, leisure and events, and it links different parts of the city. Coastal development can improve quality of life and add retail and tourism hubs, but it requires ongoing investment in maintenance, accessibility and environmental management. The Caspian itself is an enclosed body of water, so its ecology is sensitive to a range of human and natural influences. Anyone considering an apartment near the sea should think beyond the view: proximity to the shore can be an advantage, but it may add maintenance demands on the building envelope, air-conditioning systems and metal components. These costs are especially important for apartments intended for rental.

Expanding beyond the centre: resort and residential areas

While central Baku has a great deal of business, cultural and administrative activity, part of the new development is happening outside the centre. Coastal areas on the Absheron Peninsula attract interest because they allow living, hospitality and leisure to be combined. The Sea Breeze development in the Nardaran area is a prominent example of this approach. It is marketed as a mixed-use environment, but each building should be assessed by its actual status: whether it is complete, which services operate, what the service charges are and what rights apartment owners have to use the facilities. Not every service shown in a rendering is necessarily available on handover day. In addition, demand for a holiday apartment is not necessarily similar to demand for an apartment near workplaces and public transport. These are two different property products, so pricing methods and income estimates should differ too.

Does urban development lead to higher apartment prices?

Developing infrastructure and amenities may make an area more attractive, but there is no automatic link between a new project and a price rise for every property nearby. An apartment's price is also affected by competing supply, build quality, exact location, ownership rights, the credit market and the purchasing power of tenants and buyers. In a market where many units with similar specifications are being built, competition can actually limit owners' pricing power. To test a claim of appreciation, compare completed transactions over time, not just prices in listings or sales brochures. It is also important to compare like with like: a low-floor studio with no view is not equivalent to a one-bedroom apartment with a balcony and open view.

How to assess the quality of infrastructure

A visit to a property should include the route to it. Check travel times at different hours, road conditions, parking, distance to shops and medical services, access to public transport and walkability. In a new residential project, ask who is responsible for maintaining common areas, how service charges are set and whether there is a fund for major repairs. It is also worth checking water and electricity supply, internet quality, sound insulation and the condition of the lifts. None of these are minor details: they affect the living experience, rentability and the owner's costs over the years.

A diversified economy: the next stage of development

Over the years Azerbaijan's government has presented efforts to expand economic activity beyond oil and gas, including transport, logistics, tourism and services. Baku plays a central role in these processes because of its concentration of institutions, companies and infrastructure. However, the success of diversification should be measured with concrete indicators: employment, private investment, output in non-energy sectors and household incomes. A property investor should examine whether an area enjoys stable demand from a local population or relies mainly on buyers hoping to profit from future appreciation. This difference can be decisive when the market slows.

Five checks for an investor interested in Baku

First, define the purpose of the purchase: living, rental income or future sale. Second, ask for a price comparison of similar apartments and note whether it is an asking price or a completed deal. Third, have an independent local lawyer check the title documents, contract, specification, payment schedule and construction status. Fourth, prepare a budget that also includes furnishing, repairs, service charges, vacant periods and possible taxes. Fifth, test a conservative scenario: what happens if rental income is lower than expected or the sale of the property is delayed? Only after such checks can you judge whether the apartment's price fits the risk.

Summary: the city is changing, but every property is judged on its own merits

Today's Baku is the result of long processes: historic trade, the oil industry, Soviet development, independence and investment in infrastructure and architecture. The new skyline is part of the story, but not all of it. For an investor, the question is not only whether Baku is developing, but whether a particular property is offered at a reasonable price, with clear rights and real demand. Combining knowledge of the history and economy with a practical review of the property allows a more considered decision.

Frequently asked questions

Is Baku a new city?

No. Baku has a long history; modern construction is another layer in its development.

When did Azerbaijan become independent?

In 1991, with the dissolution of the Soviet Union.

What are the Flame Towers?

Three modern towers that are among the city's best-known symbols.

Is Sea Breeze in central Baku?

No. The development is in the Nardaran area, north of the city centre.

Does new construction guarantee appreciation?

No. Supply, demand, actual transactions and the property's characteristics must be examined.
